# Design Note 04

# Post-Quantum Cryptography: The Migration Challenge

> "Inventing a better lock is easy.
>
> Replacing every lock in an entire country is the difficult part."

---

# The Obvious Question

At this point, one question naturally comes up.

If RSA and ECC are expected to become vulnerable,

why don't we simply replace them?

Problem solved.

Unfortunately...

cryptography is deeply embedded into almost every modern system.

Replacing it is not like changing a library version.

It is closer to replacing the foundations of a building while people are still living inside it.

---

# Enter Post-Quantum Cryptography

Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to remain secure even against large-scale quantum computers.

Notice something.

Post-Quantum Cryptography is **not** quantum cryptography.

The names sound similar,

but they are completely different fields.

Quantum Cryptography uses the laws of quantum mechanics.

Post-Quantum Cryptography runs on ordinary computers.

Your laptop.

Your phone.

Your server.

The goal is simply to replace algorithms like RSA and ECC with new algorithms that are believed to resist both classical and quantum attacks.

---

# The Good News

Researchers have already spent decades designing candidate algorithms.

After years of evaluation,

NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) began standardizing several Post-Quantum algorithms.

Examples include

- ML-KEM (formerly Kyber)
- ML-DSA (formerly Dilithium)
- SLH-DSA (formerly SPHINCS+)

These algorithms are gradually becoming the future replacement for today's public-key cryptography.

So...

problem solved?

Not yet.

---

# The Real Problem

Imagine your organization has

- 900 repositories
- 20 years of legacy software
- 8 programming languages
- thousands of servers
- millions of users

Someone now tells you

> "Replace RSA."

Where do you even begin?

---

# Migration Is Not One Task

Migration is actually hundreds of smaller tasks.

You need to know

- Which algorithms are being used?
- Which versions?
- Which libraries?
- Which certificates?
- Which applications?
- Which teams own them?
- Which systems are internet-facing?
- Which systems process sensitive information?
- Which ones can wait?
- Which ones cannot?

Without those answers,

migration becomes guesswork.

---

# Think Like a City Planner

Suppose a city decides

> "Every bridge built before 1970 must be replaced."

Question.

Do they immediately begin construction?

No.

First they create an inventory.

Where are the bridges?

Who uses them?

How old are they?

Which ones carry the most traffic?

Which ones are already damaged?

Only then can they prioritize.

Cryptographic migration works exactly the same way.

---

# The Hidden Complexity

One application rarely uses only one cryptographic algorithm.

Consider a web application.

It may use

```
HTTPS

↓

TLS Certificate

↓

RSA

↓

AES

↓

SHA-256

↓

JWT Signing

↓

Database Encryption

↓

VPN

↓

SSH
```

Already,

one application contains multiple cryptographic assets.

Now multiply that by hundreds of applications.

---

# The Human Problem

Technology is only half the challenge.

Large organizations also need to answer

Who owns this repository?

Who maintains this certificate?

Who approved this configuration?

Can this application be upgraded?

Is the vendor still supporting it?

Some systems may not have been modified in ten years.

Some teams may no longer exist.

Some applications may not even have source code available.

Migration quickly becomes an organizational problem,

not just a technical one.

---

# Prioritization

Not every asset needs immediate replacement.

Suppose two systems exist.

System A

Processes military communications.

System B

Hosts a public cafeteria menu.

Both use RSA.

Should they receive the same priority?

Obviously not.

Migration requires prioritization.

Risk.

Business impact.

Data lifetime.

Operational constraints.

All influence the order.

---

# Mosca's Algorithm

One of the concepts mentioned in the SIH problem statement is

**Mosca's Algorithm.**

Despite its name,

it is not really an algorithm in the computer science sense.

It is better understood as a decision framework.

The basic idea is surprisingly simple.

Ask three questions.

---

## Question One

How long must the data remain confidential?

This is often called

**Shelf Life**.

Medical records

might require decades.

Government secrets

even longer.

A temporary login token

may only matter for minutes.

---

## Question Two

How long will migration take?

Some systems can migrate in weeks.

Others require years.

Large enterprises may require enormous coordination.

This is called

**Migration Time.**

---

## Question Three

When are cryptographically relevant quantum computers expected to arrive?

Nobody knows the exact answer.

Engineers make estimates based on current research.

This becomes the

**Threat Timeline.**

---

Now compare them.

If

```
Data Lifetime

+

Migration Time

>

Expected Quantum Timeline
```

then waiting becomes risky.

Preparation should begin immediately.

That is the intuition behind Mosca's framework.

---

# This Is Why Discovery Matters

Notice something.

Mosca's framework assumes we already know

- where cryptography exists
- which algorithms are used
- who owns them
- how important they are

Without discovery,

we cannot even calculate migration priorities.

Discovery is therefore the foundation of everything else.

---

# What SIH Is Actually Asking Us To Build

Read the problem statement carefully.

It does **not** ask us to migrate organizations.

It asks us to prepare them for migration.

That preparation consists of four major stages.

```
Discover

↓

Inventory

↓

Assess

↓

Recommend
```

That is exactly what Carcinus aims to automate.

---

# Engineering Discussion

Many people hear the phrase

"Post-Quantum Cryptography"

and immediately think

"new encryption."

Interestingly,

most organizations are not struggling to *choose* new algorithms.

Standards are already emerging.

Their real struggle is much more basic.

They do not know

where the old algorithms are.

Engineers often call this an observability problem.

You cannot manage what you cannot observe.

---

---

# Things To Remember

• Post-Quantum Cryptography replaces vulnerable public-key algorithms.

• PQC runs on ordinary computers.

• The challenge is not inventing new algorithms.

• The challenge is migrating existing infrastructure.

• Migration begins with discovery.

• Organizations cannot prioritize assets they cannot see.

• Mosca's framework helps estimate migration urgency.

• SIH 26164 is fundamentally an observability and inventory problem.

• Carcinus exists to make cryptographic assets visible.
