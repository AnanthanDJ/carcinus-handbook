# Design Note 03

# Quantum Computing: Why the Rules Are Changing

> "Quantum computers are not simply faster computers.
>
> They solve certain classes of problems in fundamentally different ways."

---

# A Common Misconception

Whenever people hear the words

> Quantum Computing

they often imagine a supercomputer that is simply...

1000×

10,000×

or even

1,000,000×

faster.

That is not what makes quantum computing revolutionary.

If that were true,

we could simply build larger classical computers.

Quantum computers are different because they approach certain mathematical problems using entirely different principles.

Most problems do **not** suddenly become easy.

Some remain just as difficult.

But a few...

become dramatically easier.

Unfortunately,

some of those problems are exactly the ones modern cryptography depends upon.

---

# Classical Computers

Everything you have ever programmed—

Python

C

Rust

Java

Go

JavaScript

—all ultimately runs on classical computers.

At the hardware level,

a classical computer stores information using

Bits.

A bit has only two possible values.

```
0

or

1
```

Every image,

video,

game,

operating system,

compiler,

and website

is ultimately built from billions of these bits.

Modern computers are incredibly powerful,

but they still follow these rules.

---

# Quantum Computers

Quantum computers introduce a different building block.

Instead of a Bit,

they use a

Qubit.

Unlike a bit,

a qubit behaves according to the laws of quantum mechanics.

The details are well beyond the scope of this handbook,

and fortunately,

we don't need them.

The important takeaway is simply this:

A quantum computer explores certain mathematical problems in ways that classical computers cannot efficiently replicate.

That changes what is considered "computationally difficult."

---

# What Makes RSA Secure?

Let's revisit Design Note 02.

RSA works because two mathematical operations have very different costs.

Creating an RSA key is easy.

Breaking it is extremely difficult.

More specifically,

RSA security relies on the practical difficulty of

**Integer Factorization.**

Suppose someone gives you

```
15
```

Finding

```
3 × 5
```

is easy.

Now imagine a number containing hundreds or thousands of digits.

Finding its prime factors becomes unimaginably difficult for classical computers.

That difficulty protects RSA.

---

# An Important Observation

Notice something.

RSA does **not** depend on secrecy of the algorithm.

Everyone knows how RSA works.

The mathematics has been public for decades.

Security comes from something else.

It comes from the assumption that

factoring extremely large numbers

takes too long.

This assumption has held for many years.

Most of today's Internet depends on it.

---

# Then Came Peter Shor

In 1994,

mathematician Peter Shor published an algorithm that changed cryptography forever.

Today it is simply known as

**Shor's Algorithm.**

Shor showed that

a sufficiently capable quantum computer

could factor large integers dramatically faster than the best known classical algorithms.

Remember.

Factoring large integers is exactly what RSA depends upon.

That means

if practical quantum computers become available,

RSA can no longer be considered secure.

---

# What Else Is Affected?

RSA is not the only algorithm based on difficult mathematics.

Another family,

called

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC),

also relies on mathematical problems that become vulnerable to Shor's Algorithm.

Examples include

ECDSA

ECDH

EdDSA (with caveats in practical migration discussions)

These algorithms are everywhere.

Modern TLS.

SSH.

VPNs.

Digital signatures.

Authentication.

Cloud infrastructure.

Code signing.

Breaking them would have enormous consequences.

---

# Does Quantum Computing Break Everything?

No.

This is probably the biggest misunderstanding surrounding Post-Quantum Cryptography.

Different algorithms rely on different mathematics.

Some become vulnerable.

Others do not.

For example,

AES

is **not** broken by Shor's Algorithm.

Hash functions like SHA-256 are also **not** broken in the same way.

Quantum algorithms can reduce the effective security of symmetric cryptography (for example through Grover's Algorithm), but the impact is much smaller and can often be addressed by using larger key sizes, such as AES-256 instead of AES-128.

The immediate concern is therefore

Public-Key Cryptography,

not symmetric encryption.

---

# Why This Matters

Think back to HTTPS.

Remember the simplified workflow.

```
Browser

↓

RSA / ECC

↓

Shared AES Key

↓

AES encrypts everything
```

If RSA or ECC can no longer safely establish the shared key,

the entire secure communication process becomes vulnerable,

even if AES itself remains strong.

Public-key cryptography acts like the front door.

AES protects the rooms inside.

If the front door can be bypassed,

the strength of the lock on the bedroom door matters much less.

---

# "Quantum Computers Don't Exist Yet"

Another common misconception.

People often say

> "We don't have quantum computers yet."

This statement is only partially true.

Quantum computers already exist.

Companies and research organizations around the world have built working quantum machines.

However,

today's machines are

- relatively small
- noisy
- error-prone

They are not yet capable of breaking modern RSA at practical scales.

But research is advancing rapidly.

Whether that milestone arrives in

10 years,

20 years,

or longer,

organizations responsible for long-lived data cannot afford to wait until the last minute.

---

# Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

Imagine an attacker today.

They intercept encrypted diplomatic communications.

Military communications.

Medical records.

Research data.

Financial information.

Can they decrypt it today?

Maybe not.

But they don't have to.

Storage is cheap.

The attacker simply archives everything.

Years later,

when sufficiently capable quantum computers become available,

they decrypt the archived data.

This strategy has a name.

**Harvest Now, Decrypt Later.**

It is one of the primary reasons governments are already planning migration.

Some information remains sensitive for decades.

Waiting until quantum computers become practical would already be too late.

---

# Why Governments Care

Not every piece of information needs protection for 30 years.

A shopping receipt probably does not.

Nuclear research might.

Military communications certainly might.

Medical histories often do.

Citizen identity records can remain valuable for decades.

The longer data must remain confidential,

the sooner migration planning should begin.

This idea becomes very important later when we discuss

Mosca's Algorithm,

which helps prioritize migration based on how long data needs to remain secure.

---

# Engineering Discussion

Notice something interesting.

Quantum computers have not yet broken RSA.

Yet organizations are already investing heavily in migration.

Why?

Because replacing cryptography is not like updating a mobile app.

It involves

finding assets

↓

understanding dependencies

↓

testing replacements

↓

certification

↓

deployment

↓

validation

↓

monitoring

Large organizations may require years to complete that process.

Migration planning therefore begins long before the threat fully materializes.

Engineering is about preparing before failure occurs.

---

# Things To Remember

• Quantum computers are fundamentally different from classical computers.

• RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring very large integers.

• Shor's Algorithm dramatically changes that assumption for sufficiently capable quantum computers.

• RSA and many elliptic-curve systems are therefore expected to require replacement.

• AES and SHA-256 are not broken in the same way.

• Public-key cryptography is the primary concern.

• Harvest Now, Decrypt Later is already motivating governments to prepare.

• Migration takes years.

• Preparation starts long before quantum computers become practical.

• Carcinus helps organizations prepare by providing visibility into their existing cryptographic assets.
